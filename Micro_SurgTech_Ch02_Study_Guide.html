What Is Microbiology?

The study of microorganisms — a diverse group of minute, simple life forms including bacteria, archaea, algae, fungi, protozoa, and viruses. The field is concerned with the structure, function, and classification of such organisms and with ways of both exploiting and controlling their activities.

Prevention of surgical site infections (SSIs) is of paramount importance in the OR environment.

What Are Microbes?
  • Also called microorganisms
  • Include microscopic living organisms AND bits of material that are not alive (viruses, prions)
  • First observed by van Leeuwenhoek's microscope
  • Modern electron microscopes allow study of the smallest pathogens including viruses
  • Comprise the largest group of Earth's inhabitants — going back ~3 billion years
Normal Flora & Microbiome
  • No living being can survive without some exposure to microorganisms — this is its normal flora or microbiome
  • When normal flora organisms escape their typical environments, they can cause serious pathological conditions
  • Transient microbes (microflora) — temporary microbes acquired by touching surfaces or environmental exposure
  • Resident / indigenous microbes — normal microflora living deep in skin layers
  • OR implication: invasive procedures require antimicrobial skin cleansing prior to incision due to both resident and transient flora

History of Microbial Classification

Scientific classification has evolved over 2,400 years — from two kingdoms to modern multi-domain systems. Each revision reflected new discoveries about the true nature of life.

~350 BCE — Aristotle
Two Kingdoms: Plantae (plants) and Animalia (animals). Used for centuries. Based on visible characteristics only.
1894 — Ernst Haeckel
Three Kingdoms: Added Protista (included both single-celled eukaryotes and bacterial prokaryotes). First recognition that some organisms fit neither plant nor animal category.
1956 — Herbert Copeland
Four Kingdoms: Split Protista into two — creating Kingdom of Bacteria, more clearly separating single-cell eukaryotes from bacteria.
1959 / 1969 — Robert Whittaker
Five Kingdoms: Plantae, Animalia, Protista (single-cell eukaryotes), Monera (single-cell prokaryotes), Fungi. Based on phenetics — scientific observation of similarities between organisms.
1977 — Carl Woese & Colleagues
Discovered Archaea — a distinct category, separate from both eukaryotes and prokaryotes, sharing a single genetic ancestor. Often found in extreme environments (glaciers, hot springs). More closely resemble eukaryotes.
1990 — Carl Woese
Three Domains System: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya. Based on cladistics and phylogeny (genetic family trees) rather than phenetics.
2010 — Comparative Genomics Researchers
Two Empires (proposed): Living Cellular Empire (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya) + Non-Living Viral Empire (viruses, plasmids, prions, transposons, viroids). Proposed because the Three Domains system did not account for non-living microbes.
Key Distinction: Earlier systems relied on phenetics (what organisms look like). Modern systems rely on cladistics and phylogeny (genetic relationships and evolutionary history) — a fundamentally different and more accurate approach.

Three Domains & Two Empires Systems

The modern classification frameworks used in microbiology today — one based on genetic evolutionary relationships, the other proposed to account for non-living viral agents.

Three Domains System (Woese, 1990)

Based on cladistics (charting genetic lineages) and phylogeny (evolutionary relationships) — creating a microbial family tree from genetic data rather than appearance.

Domain: Bacteria
  • Prokaryotic cells
  • Genetic material in protoplasm (no nucleus)
  • Most numerous organisms on Earth
  • Includes all bacteria
Domain: Archaea
  • Single-celled, similar structure to bacteria
  • Distinctly different genetic structure
  • Affinity for extreme environments
  • Salt marshes, steam vents, ice glaciers
  • Likely ancient microbial precursor to both groups
Domain: Eukarya
  • Cells have membrane-bound organelles
  • Nucleus containing DNA
  • Includes humans, animals, plants, fungi, protozoa, algae

Two Empires System (Proposed 2010)

Empire 1: Cellular Organisms (Living)

Includes all members of the Three Domains system:

  • Bacteria (prokaryotes)
  • Archaea (ancient extremophiles)
  • Eukarya (eukaryotes — plants, animals, fungi, protozoa, algae)
Empire 2: Viral Agents (Non-Living)

A separate empire accounting for non-living agents — not represented in the Three Domains system:

  • Viruses
  • Plasmids
  • Prions
  • Transposons
  • Viroids
  • Other genetic material particles

The empire of viruses far exceeds the numbers of microbes in the cellular empire.

Why Two Empires? The Three Domains system was challenged by comparative genomics researchers in 2010 because it did not represent non-living viral agents. Genetic nucleotide sequencing showed these agents formed their own distinct group — leading to the proposed Two Empires framework separating cellular life from non-living viral agents.

Phenetics vs. Cladistics — Key Distinction

Phenetics (Older Method)

Classification based on observable similarities between organisms — what they look like, how they behave, structural features. Used through the Five Kingdoms era.

Cladistics & Phylogeny (Modern Method)

Classification based on genetic relationships and evolutionary history. Uses DNA/RNA sequencing to map microbial family trees and trace common ancestors. Used in the Three Domains system onward.

The Cellular Empire — Eukaryotes, Prokaryotes & Archaea

All living microorganisms in the cellular empire fall into three major categories, each with distinct cellular structure and characteristics.

🧠 Eukaryotes
Cells have membrane-bound organelles and a nucleus containing DNA

Multi-celled Eukaryotes:

  • Humans
  • Animals
  • Birds
  • Insects
  • Plants

Single-celled Eukaryotes:

  • Protozoa
  • Algae
  • Fungi
🦠 Prokaryotes
Cells contain genetic material in protoplasm — no membrane-bound nucleus
  • Includes all bacteria
  • Genetic material (DNA/RNA) floats freely in the cytoplasm (protoplasm)
  • Generally simpler in structure than eukaryotes
  • Cyanobacteria — once thought to be algae; are actually aquatic bacteria that perform photosynthesis. Also known as blue-green algae.
  • Most numerous organisms on Earth
🌌 Archaea (Archaebacteria)
Single-celled organisms — similar structure to bacteria but distinctly different genetics
  • Discovered by Carl Woese and colleagues in 1977
  • Separate category from both eukaryotes and prokaryotes
  • Share a single genetic ancestor — likely the ancient microbial precursor to both eukaryotes and prokaryotes
  • More closely resemble eukaryotes genetically despite bacteria-like structure
  • Extremophile affinity — thrive in harsh environments:
Salt Marshes
High salinity environments
Steam / Hot Vents
Extreme heat environments
Ice Glaciers
Extreme cold environments

The Virus Empire — Viruses, Viroids & Prions

Non-living agents that require living hosts to replicate. They make up the second empire in the Two Empires classification system and far outnumber all cellular organisms combined.

🦠 Viruses
  • Extremely small bundles of DNA or RNA (retroviruses) — visible only with an electron microscope
  • Nucleic acids wrapped in a capsid — a protein coating made of capsomeres
  • Non-living, acellular — do not have the capacity to perform reproductive functions on their own
  • Classified as obligate intracellular parasites — must invade a host cell and use its reproductive machinery to replicate and spread viral DNA/RNA
  • Penetrate healthy cells, becoming parasites, spreading during cellular reproductive processes
🧮 Viroids
  • Thought to be precursors to modern viruses
  • A fraction of the size of viruses
  • Naked loops of RNA — no protein capsid
  • Once inside a cell, invade cellular RNA
  • One of the viral empire members included in the Two Empires classification

Prions — Non-Living Agents of Fatal CNS Disease

What Are Prions?
  • Non-living agents responsible for incurable, fatal diseases of the central nervous system
  • Cause several forms of Transmissible Spongiform Encephalopathies (TSEs)
  • Normal proteins (PrPc) in neural tissue fold abnormally, creating deformed PrPsc proteins
  • Deformed proteins influence other normal proteins to also deform — a self-propagating cascade
  • Progression destroys neural tissue, creating scattered holes — giving brain matter a sponge-like appearance
  • No nucleic acids, no cellular structure — cannot be classified in the Three Domains system

Prion Diseases by Species

Human Prion Diseases
  • Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease (CJD) — most common human prion disease; rapidly fatal dementia
  • Variant CJD (vCJD) — linked to consumption of BSE-infected cattle ("mad cow")
  • Gerstmann-Straussler-Scheinker syndrome (GSS) — rare genetically transmitted; causes ataxia and dementia
  • Fatal Familial Insomnia (FFI) — rare genetic disease; disrupts sleep-wakefulness patterns
  • Kuru — TSE caused by consumption of prion-infected human brain tissue; linked to now-discontinued burial rituals in New Guinea
Animal Prion Diseases (TSEs)
  • Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy (BSE) — "mad cow disease" in cattle
  • Ovine Spongiform Encephalopathy (Scrapie) — in sheep
  • Chronic Wasting Disease (CWD) — in hooved animals (deer, elk, moose) in North America

OR Connection: Prions are highly resistant to standard sterilization. Special instrument processing protocols apply for suspected prion cases (e.g., craniotomy for CJD evaluation).

Board Exam Note: Prions are not classified in the Three Domains system (Bacteria/Archaea/Eukarya) because they are non-living and have no cellular structure or nucleic acids. They belong to the Viral Empire in the Two Empires classification. This distinction is clinically significant because standard sterilization methods do NOT destroy prions.

Taxonomy & Nomenclature

Taxonomy provides a universal language for identifying, naming, and classifying microorganisms — enabling accurate communication between scientists worldwide and supporting clinical diagnosis and treatment.

Taxonomy

The system of naming every living organism. Objectives:

  • Establishing relationships between like organisms
  • Differentiating between different groups of organisms
  • Classifying previously unknown organisms
  • Allows accurate medical diagnosis and prescribed treatment
  • Universal communication language among scientists worldwide
Binomial Nomenclature (Linnaeus, mid-1700s)
  • Created by Carolus Linnaeus in the mid-1700s
  • Names written in Latin (language of science in the 1700s)
  • Two-name system: Genus + species
  • Genus name: Always capitalized, spelled out first time, then abbreviated (e.g., S. aureus)
  • Species name: Always lowercase, fully spelled out
  • Handwritten: underlined  |  Typewritten: italicized
  • Example: Staphylococcus aureus then S. aureus

Taxonomic Hierarchy

Subdivisions from largest (most general) to smallest (most specific):

KingdomLargest group — broadest classification (e.g., Bacteria, Animalia)
PhylumAlso called "Division" in plant/fungi taxonomy
ClassMajor subdivision of a phylum
OrderGroup of related families
FamilyGroup of closely related genera
GenusClosely related species group — first name in binomial nomenclature (capitalized, italicized)
SpeciesMost specific — the most closely related group of organisms — second name in binomial nomenclature (lowercase, italicized)

Mnemonic: Kind People Come Over For Good Soup

Prokaryote Reference: The specific taxonomic classification for prokaryotes is Bergey's Manual of Systematic Bacteriology — the standardized reference for microbiology researchers. Continuously revised and updated electronically through the Bergey's Manual Trust.

Viral Taxonomy

Viruses Are Classified By:
  • Morphology (structure/appearance)
  • Nucleic acid type (DNA or RNA)
  • Mode of replication
  • Types of host organisms invaded
  • Types of diseases they cause

General host-based classifications: Zoophaginae animals   Phytophaginae plants   Phaginae prokaryotes

ICTV — International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses
  • Governs viral taxonomy internationally
  • 2021 ICTV 10th Report: nine groups of viruses based on genetic structures
  • Common viral names are not italicized
  • Genus names: italicized, end in -virus (e.g., Paramyxovirus)
  • Sub-family: ends in -virinae (e.g., Paramyxovirinae)
  • Family: ends in -viridae (e.g., Paramyxoviridae)
  • Order: ends in -virales (e.g., Picornavirales)
Baltimore Classification System
  • Devised by virologist and Nobel Laureate David Baltimore
  • 7 groups based on genome and messenger RNA (mRNA)
  • Viruses carry single or double genetic strands of DNA or RNA
  • Considers positive (+) or negative (−) sense component
  • Involves viral mRNA's ability to interact with host cell cytoplasm (translation → proteins/enzymes for replication)
  • ICTV follows the Baltimore system, adding subviral agents and unclassified viruses

Eukaryotes
Click to reveal
Organisms whose cells have membrane-bound organelles and a true nucleus containing DNA. Includes humans, animals, plants, fungi, protozoa, and algae. One of the three domains in Woese's classification system.
Genome
Click to reveal
The complete set of genetic information of an organism or virus — the entirety of its DNA or RNA. The Baltimore classification system classifies viruses based on their genome type.
Microbiome
Click to reveal
The entire community of microorganisms (bacteria, fungi, viruses) that inhabit a particular environment — especially those living in or on the human body. No living organism can survive without some relationship with its microbiome.
Microbiology
Click to reveal
The study of microorganisms — a diverse group of minute, simple life forms including bacteria, archaea, algae, fungi, protozoa, and viruses. Concerned with structure, function, classification, and ways to exploit and control microbial activity.
Morphology
Click to reveal
The study of the structure and form of organisms. Morphology is one of the criteria used to classify viruses — along with nucleic acid type, mode of replication, host type, and disease caused.
Normal Flora
Click to reveal
Microorganisms that normally inhabit the body without causing disease. Every organism has a normal flora (microbiome). When normal flora escapes its typical environment (e.g., through surgery or injury), it can cause serious pathological conditions.
Obligate Intracellular Parasite
Click to reveal
An organism or agent (such as a virus) that can only reproduce inside a living host cell. Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites — they hijack the host cell's reproductive machinery to replicate their own genetic material.
Osmotrophic
Click to reveal
Obtaining nutrients by absorbing dissolved organic compounds from the environment — characteristic of fungi. Fungi are classified as osmotrophic eukaryotes in Whittaker's Five Kingdoms system.
Pathological Conditions
Click to reveal
Disease states caused by pathogens or abnormal processes. Even normal flora organisms can cause serious pathological conditions when they escape their typical environment — for example, gut bacteria entering the bloodstream during surgery.
Phenetics
Click to reveal
Classification of organisms based on observable physical similarities and characteristics — appearance, structure, behavior. Used in earlier classification systems up through Whittaker's Five Kingdoms. Replaced/supplemented by cladistics and phylogeny in modern systems.
Phylogeny
Click to reveal
The study of evolutionary relationships among organisms based on genetic data. Phylogenetic analysis creates a microbial "family tree" tracing common ancestors across billions of years. Used in Woese's Three Domains classification.
Prions
Click to reveal
Non-living, misfolded proteins (PrPsc) that cause normal neural proteins (PrPc) to also misfold in a self-propagating cascade. Cause fatal transmissible spongiform encephalopathies (TSEs) including CJD in humans and BSE in cattle. Resistant to standard sterilization methods.
Prokaryotes
Click to reveal
Organisms whose cells contain genetic material in the protoplasm but lack a membrane-bound nucleus. Includes all bacteria. Simpler in structure than eukaryotes. Most numerous organisms on Earth.
Protoplasm
Click to reveal
The living contents of a cell — the cytoplasm and nucleus together. In prokaryotes, genetic material floats freely in the protoplasm rather than being enclosed in a membrane-bound nucleus.
Retroviruses
Click to reveal
Viruses that carry their genetic information as RNA and use the enzyme reverse transcriptase to convert their RNA into DNA inside the host cell. HIV is the most well-known retrovirus. Classified as RNA viruses in the Baltimore classification system.
Taxonomic Hierarchy
Click to reveal
The system of subdivisions for classifying all living organisms, from largest to smallest group: Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species. Mnemonic: "Kind People Come Over For Good Soup."
Taxonomy
Click to reveal
The scientific system of naming, classifying, and organizing every living organism. Establishes universal standardized communication among scientists and supports accurate medical diagnosis and treatment.

A group of fatal, progressive neurodegenerative diseases caused by prions. Characterized by abnormal protein folding that destroys neural tissue, creating sponge-like holes in the brain. Examples: CJD (humans), BSE/mad cow disease (cattle), scrapie (sheep), chronic wasting disease (deer/elk).

Thought to be precursors to modern viruses. Extremely small naked loops of RNA — smaller than viruses and lacking a protein capsid. Once inside a cell, they invade cellular RNA. Members of the Viral Empire in the Two Empires classification.
Viruses

Non-living, acellular microbes consisting of DNA or RNA enclosed in a protein capsid. Obligate intracellular parasites that must use a living host cell's reproductive machinery to replicate. Only visible with an electron microscope.

1. Which scientist first proposed the Three Domains classification system based on cladistics and phylogeny?

2. Viruses are classified as obligate intracellular parasites because they:

✓ Correct! Viruses are non-living and acellular — they cannot replicate on their own. They must invade a host cell and use its reproductive processes to copy their DNA or RNA, making them obligate intracellular parasites.
✗ Incorrect. Viruses are classified as obligate intracellular parasites because they are non-living and acellular — they must hijack a living host cell's reproductive machinery to replicate their genetic material.

3. In binomial nomenclature, which of the following is written correctly?

✗ Incorrect. Binomial nomenclature rules: Genus name is capitalized (Staphylococcus), species name is lowercase (aureus). Typewritten names are italicized. So the correct form is Staphylococcus aureus.

4. What is the correct order of the taxonomic hierarchy from largest (most general) to smallest (most specific)?

✓ Correct! The taxonomic hierarchy from largest to smallest: Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species. Mnemonic: "Kind People Come Over For Good Soup."
✗ Incorrect. The correct order from largest to smallest is: Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species. Mnemonic: "Kind People Come Over For Good Soup."

5. Prions cause disease by which mechanism?

✓ Correct! Prions are misfolded proteins (PrPsc) that cause normal neural proteins (PrPc) to also misfold in a self-propagating cascade. This destroys neural tissue and creates sponge-like holes in brain matter — hence the name "spongiform encephalopathy."
✗ Incorrect. Prions cause disease by triggering a chain reaction of protein misfolding: abnormal PrPsc proteins cause normal PrPc proteins to misfold, progressively destroying neural tissue and creating a sponge-like appearance in brain matter.

6. The Two Empires classification system was proposed in 2010 primarily because:

✓ Correct! Comparative genomics researchers proposed the Two Empires system because the Three Domains system (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya) did not represent non-living viral agents. The viral empire — which far exceeds cellular organisms in number — needed its own category.
✗ Incorrect. The Two Empires system was proposed because the Three Domains system excluded non-living viral agents (viruses, prions, viroids, plasmids, transposons). These are acellular and non-living, requiring a separate empire from cellular life.

7. Which of the following correctly describes Archaea?

✓ Correct! Archaea are single-celled organisms that look like bacteria structurally but have distinctly different genetic structures. They thrive in extreme environments (salt marshes, steam vents, ice glaciers) and are thought to be the ancient precursor to both eukaryotes and prokaryotes.
✗ Incorrect. Archaea are single-celled organisms structurally similar to bacteria but genetically distinct. They are often found in extreme environments and are likely the ancient common ancestor of both prokaryotes and eukaryotes.

8. The Baltimore classification system categorizes viruses into seven groups based on:

✓ Correct! David Baltimore — virologist and Nobel Laureate — devised a system classifying viruses into 7 groups based on their genome (DNA or RNA, single or double stranded) and messenger RNA. The ICTV follows this system.
✗ Incorrect. The Baltimore classification system groups viruses into 7 categories based on genome type (DNA vs. RNA, single vs. double stranded) and their messenger RNA (mRNA), including whether there is a positive or negative sense component.

9. In the surgical environment, which aspect of normal flora is most clinically significant for the surgical technologist?

✓ Correct! Normal flora (resident and transient microflora) can cause serious infections when introduced into a surgical site. This is why pre-op skin cleansing with antimicrobial agents and strict sterile technique are essential — not to eliminate all microbes, but to reduce the microbial load to the lowest possible number.
✗ Incorrect. Normal flora becomes clinically significant when it escapes its typical environment. During invasive procedures, skin flora can contaminate the surgical site and cause serious infections. This is why antimicrobial skin prep and sterile technique are critical.

✓ Correct! Bergey's Manual of Systematic Bacteriology is the specific taxonomic classification reference for prokaryotes. It is continuously revised and updated electronically through the Bergey's Manual Trust and serves as the standardized reference for microbiology researchers worldwide.
✗ Incorrect. Bergey's Manual of Systematic Bacteriology is the standardized taxonomic classification reference for prokaryotes (bacteria). It is maintained by the Bergey's Manual Trust and continuously updated.
